# Digital Platforms Under Pressure: What Australia's Digital Duty of Care Reveals About the Future of the Internet

{{ article_hero(page) }}

{{ article_summary(page) }}

{{ article_takeaways(page) }}

Australia has become one of the world's leading laboratories for digital regulation. From its widely discussed social media age restrictions to stronger online safety powers, the country has consistently challenged the idea that technology companies should largely regulate themselves. Its recently proposed Digital Duty of Care framework takes that approach further by requiring online services to take reasonable steps to prevent foreseeable online harm.

The results of Australia's regulatory efforts have been mixed. Supporters argue they have elevated important conversations about online harm, child protection and platform accountability. Critics question their effectiveness in a global digital environment and warn of potential impacts on free expression and innovation.

Now Australia is taking what could be its most significant step yet. The proposed Digital Duty of Care may prove more influential than previous reforms because it focuses less on content and more on the systems, algorithms and design choices that shape user behaviour.

## Australia's regulatory experiment

Australia has increasingly positioned itself at the forefront of digital regulation, and become a leader in taking meaningful legislative action at a Government level among leading democracies. While many countries have focused on privacy, competition or content moderation, Australian policymakers have centred their attention on a more fundamental question:

**What responsibility should digital platforms bear for the experiences they create?**

<aside class="de-key-term">
  <strong>Key term: Digital Duty of Care</strong>
  <p>A proposed Australian framework requiring online services to take reasonable steps to prevent foreseeable online harm. Importantly, and many would say crucially, it extends beyond social media to include AI-enabled services and recommendation technologies.</p>
</aside>

In practice, the framework would ask providers to do four things.

{{ story_steps(page, "duty_steps") }}

The legislation reflects a key lesson from previous regulatory efforts: online harm rarely stems from a single piece of content. More often, it is an amalgamation and cumulative build-up from the interaction of algorithms, incentives, platform design and user behaviour. Which means the question regulators are asking has changed.

<div class="de-before-after">
  <article>
    <span>The question used to be</span>
    <h3>How do we remove harmful content?</h3>
    <p>Treats harm as a post to be found and taken down, after the fact.</p>
  </article>
  <article>
    <span>The question is now</span>
    <h3>How do we prevent systems from amplifying harm in the first place?</h3>
    <p>Treats harm as something a platform's design can make more or less likely.</p>
  </article>
</div>

The Digital Duty of Care is Australia's clearest attempt yet to answer that question.

## Could the UK be next?

For UK businesses and policymakers, Australia's latest move may offer a preview of what comes next. The UK has already demonstrated its willingness to follow Australia's lead on online safety.

{{ story_facts(page, "uk_facts") }}

The UK has also committed to learning from Australia's implementation experience and outcomes. This is interesting, as despite the obvious geographical distance between the two nations, there is an undeniable historic and cultural connection. In simple terms, the impact or lack of impact of this legislation could well be an indicator of its viability and benefit within the UK. It can be assumed that the UK Government may well look on with a vested interest!

What's particularly interesting is that early evidence from Australia's social media restrictions has been mixed. While some reductions in underage usage have been observed, implementation challenges and workarounds have highlighted the limitations of focusing solely on access restrictions. These early findings are a strong argument for the creation of a broader duty-of-care model.

<section class="de-callout-grid">
  <article>
    <h3>Age restrictions</h3>
    <p>Focus on <strong>who</strong> can access a platform.</p>
  </article>
  <article>
    <h3>Digital Duty of Care</h3>
    <p>Focuses on <strong>how</strong> the platform itself operates.</p>
  </article>
</section>

Together, they represent two sides of the same objective: reducing foreseeable harm in digital environments.

## From content moderation to systems accountability

One of the most notable features of the legislation is its focus on platform architecture rather than individual posts. This approach mirrors practices in industries such as aviation, healthcare and engineering. When incidents happen repeatedly, investigators rarely focus solely on individuals. Instead, they examine the systems that contributed to the outcome. In aviation, recent airport disruptions in the UK have indeed been pointed toward the underlying systems employed.

The same principle is now being applied to digital platforms. Rather than asking whether harmful content was removed quickly enough, policymakers are increasingly asking whether a platform's design made the harm more likely to occur.

<p class="de-reflection"><strong>Reflection:</strong> If you were investigating a harm on a platform you use every day, would you look at the post, or at the system that put it in front of you?</p>

## The algorithm question

At the heart of the legislation is a growing scrutiny of recommendation systems. For years, algorithms have been viewed as neutral technologies designed to show users relevant content. Yet recommendation engines optimise for engagement, not wellbeing.

Algorithms do not understand truth. They do not understand harm. They understand signals.

<div class="de-chip-list" aria-label="The signals algorithms optimise for">

Under the proposed reforms, users would gain more control over algorithmically curated feeds, while platforms would assume greater responsibility for the impact of those systems. Which raises an important question.

## Lessons for artificial intelligence

The implications extend well beyond social media. The framework includes AI systems and other emerging digital services, recognising that safety considerations should apply to recommendation engines, generative AI and automated decision-making systems. As AI becomes embedded into search, customer service, education and marketing, a new debate is emerging.

<div class="de-before-after">
  <article>
    <span>Organisations used to ask</span>
    <h3>Can AI do this task?</h3>
    <p>A question about capability, answered by the people building and buying the technology.</p>
  </article>
  <article>
    <span>Regulators, customers and society now ask</span>
    <h3>What happens when AI gets it wrong?</h3>
    <p>A question about consequences, answered by whoever ends up accountable for them.</p>
  </article>
</div>

The Digital Duty of Care reflects a wider shift from focusing purely on technological capability to considering foreseeable consequences. Innovation alone is no longer enough.

Accountability is becoming part of the product.

## A new definition of digital responsibility

Perhaps the most interesting aspect of the proposal is that it applies a centuries-old concept to modern technology.

{{ story_pull_quote(page) }}

Doctors, teachers and employers all have a duty of care because they create environments that affect others, and therefore carry responsibility for foreseeable risks. Australia is effectively asking whether digital platforms should be held to a similar standard in a world where billions of people spend significant portions of their lives online.

## What this means for marketing professionals

For marketers, these developments are about far more than compliance. Success has traditionally been measured through engagement, reach and conversions. However, as regulators increasingly scrutinise algorithms and digital experiences, ethical considerations may become just as important as performance metrics.

Tomorrow's marketers will need to understand not only how platforms influence behaviour, but also how technology can be used responsibly.

{{ story_checklist(page, "skills_checklist") }}

What was once largely a compliance issue is becoming a commercial and marketing responsibility. This is why developing digital capability has never been more important.

{{ story_cta(page) }}

As expectations around digital responsibility continue to evolve, the organisations that succeed may be those investing in marketers who can balance innovation, performance and accountability.

## The bigger question

Whether Australia's latest intervention succeeds remains to be seen. However, its significance may lie less in achieving perfect outcomes and more in its willingness to tackle difficult questions publicly. Like the social media restrictions before it, the Digital Duty of Care will generate debate around effectiveness, enforcement and unintended consequences. Yet if it influences how other countries think about algorithms, AI and platform accountability, Australia may once again find itself shaping the global conversation.

For decades, the technology sector has measured success through growth, engagement and scale. The Digital Duty of Care introduces a different metric.

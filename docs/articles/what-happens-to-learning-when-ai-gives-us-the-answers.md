## It's cold, and it's dark and I have to go to the gym. Anyone?

Recently, the clocks went back and, whilst I was very grateful for the initial extra hour in bed, I was quickly missing the summer (which I also moaned about) as we plunged into darker, colder evenings.

Going to the gym after work was not so difficult during the heat of a record-breaking summer, largely because: 1) the gym has aircon, 2) the gym has a pool, 3) my house very much does not have aircon or a pool. Lately, the motivation to go feels much harder to find.

But, as they say, we move!

In spite of this, I have been getting into the gym and, as I went the other day, I thought, this isn't something AI can do for me, is it?

I can get AI to build me a workout programme, analyse my performance, give me reminders and even motivational phrases to keep me going. But the physical and cognitive benefits of doing the work are mine alone. Sure, I could ask my personal trainer to do the exercises for me (sorry Ben!) and my session would technically be completed without me even putting my trainers on. But it definitely wouldn't be my muscles getting any stronger.

Buoyed by the post-workout high after a particularly sweaty session earlier this week, it got me wondering if learning is a lot more like exercise than we think?

Going to the gym to lift heavy things is not something we choose to do because it seems the most efficient way to move them from A to B. We do it because the effort itself gives us something back.

## What effort are we asking AI to take from our lives?

In education, much of the conversation has been focused on whether using AI constitutes cheating, all the while, educators continue to try to find the balance between embracing the tools of today and still being able to assess learning that has actually happened inside a human brain.

<p class="de-article-interjection">What if some of the work we're trying to avoid is actually where the learning happens?</p>

As a kid I remember the frustration of being told to show my working out. I had the answer so why did it matter how I got there? Of course, that was exactly the point. The working out showed my thinking and that I understood something. It also provided the teacher with an opportunity to help me understand where I went wrong.

Our additional effort is sometimes not useful or productive though. I'm more than happy for AI to take away repetitive work admin and formatting, or to save me from spending twenty minutes trying to find something I know exists somewhere. But some friction is worthwhile.

When you get stuck on a problem and have to find a different way to do it. When you're writing something and the message you're trying to get across just isn't quite coming through clearly enough. In a world increasingly focused on productivity, these things can feel inefficient and worth offloading to AI.

<p class="de-article-interjection">But… maybe efficiency doesn't always have to be the goal?</p>

When I was studying for my Data Science Masters, we weren't taught to put data into a machine learning model and blindly accept whatever came out the other side. Quite the opposite. We needed to understand enough to question its outputs. What assumptions have we made? What bias could come from the data? How confident are we in the result? And, pretty importantly, when might it be wrong?

If a model used to identify cancer from medical imaging is 95% accurate, for example, that isn't enough information on its own. What happens in the other 5% of cases?

It's important we understand how we got to an answer just as much as having the answer in the first place. It's something instilled in us in education settings but it shouldn't end there.

## Cognitive atrophy

One of the potential risks here is cognitive atrophy. So much of what we know is learnt by doing. We try things, get things wrong, we ask questions and we observe and learn from others more experienced than us. We know something mostly because we have experienced it. Some of that knowledge is tacit.

And that presents us with a kind of awkward productivity problem.

If AI can do early career work faster, cheaper and perhaps sometimes better, why wouldn't we let it?

This feels important when we think about apprentices. The whole point of an apprenticeship is that learning and doing happen alongside one another. Apprentices develop judgement, confidence and professional instincts by actually doing the work, asking questions, getting things wrong and trying again.

Of course, this does not mean we should have apprentices learn every task of past dated roles and ways of working. Jobs and tech changes, and so learning should change with them. There would be little value in preserving outdated work just because it's how expertise was once developed.

But, if AI removes some of the work through which people traditionally developed judgement and confidence, surely we need to think deliberately about what is going to replace that learning journey?

I've seen myself the value that apprentices bring to organisations whilst on their learning journeys. They offer different perspectives and often ask questions that other people might not have considered. If we want innovation in organisations, we need people to challenge the way things have always been done.

This all matters in an age of AI. For all that AI can do, there is still value in a person coming across a problem with fresh eyes, and some organisational context, and asking, "Why do we do it like that?"

If we automate too much of the work through which people learn without thinking about what replaces it, we potentially lose something twice:

In all of our discussions about improving outputs and efficiency savings, maybe productivity is not the only thing we should be optimising for. As much as it pains me to admit it, I do get satisfaction from seeing progress at the gym as the weights get heavier. We get satisfaction from developing ourselves and getting good at something.

## So where do we still need to do some of the heavy lifting ourselves?

As the Nobel Prize recently shared, Medicine Laureate Craig Mello simply said:

{{ story_pull_quote(page, "mello_quote") }}

That is science in a nutshell, right?

If I use AI simply to make what I already believe sound more convincing, I'm not learning anything new. Scale that up, and perhaps we risk something bigger: using these enormously powerful tools to reinforce what we already think rather than discover what we don't yet know. Going back to what my Data Science professors taught me, as more of us interact with AI models, we all need to get more comfortable interrogating the answers they give us.

And so we come back to the gym workout (the one I'm currently procrastinating on)…

I don't particularly enjoy leaving my warm house on a cold, dark evening after a long day at work. There are points during a workout when I would be very happy for absolutely anyone to take over a few reps for me. And given the opportunity, my brain can and will produce a great many reasons why tonight probably isn't the night to show up.

But.

I like getting stronger.

And unfortunately, there doesn't seem to be a shortcut where somebody else can do that bit for me.

So maybe learning isn't so different. Maybe there are some things where we should welcome every shortcut available to us, and others where removing the struggle risks removing something human that we actually wanted, or perhaps needed, to gain from the process.

As we get better at building machines that can do more of the thinking, writing, analysing and problem-solving for us, I reckon one of the more important questions won't be

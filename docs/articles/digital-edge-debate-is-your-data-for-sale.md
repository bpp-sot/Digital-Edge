# Digital Edge Debates: Is Your Data for Sale?

{{ article_hero(page) }}

{{ article_summary(page) }}

{{ article_takeaways(page) }}

Last week we hosted our second Digital Edge debate, and I for one was thrilled to feel just as challenged and inspired as I did by the first.

Through our Digital Edge debates, we aim to be intentionally controversial, challenging our learners to research and defend an argument they may not necessarily agree with. For this debate, we landed on the topic of data monetisation and the use of our data by large tech corporations.

Right from the start of opening arguments, each team made their mark and showcased how well prepared and passionate they were about the debate. Toby from team one started us off with a core component of the team's upcoming arguments: that to keep the internet free and accessible to use, users need to accept a level of monetisation. Kelly from team two then followed, outlining that the exchange of services for data needs to be clear and fully informed, which team two argue it is not.

This time, we planned the debate around three key topics, to guide our learners along argument lines and allow for researched rebuttals on both sides:

<div class="de-chip-list" aria-label="The three debate topics">
  <span>Is paying with our data a fair trade for free services?</span>
  <span>How does the growth of AI and digital innovation, and its associated costs, impact the demand for data monetisation?</span>
  <span>Is it right to have such high expectations of privacy, at no user cost?</span>
</div>

Each team had divided these topics between them to focus their research and planned how their arguments would build on each other throughout the debate.

## Team one: keeping the internet free

Team one centred some of their argument around the accessibility and democracy of the internet, and how this would not be the case if users paid for services rather than income streams coming from monetising user data. Lily and Seb also pointed out that as well as being users, we also benefit from how Gen AI tools learn and train on the data we input, refining answers and giving us better results in turn. To summarise, Eileen added that "by not allowing data monetisation, it's a huge issue for democracy."

## Team two: an uninformed exchange

For me, some of the most interesting arguments from team two centred on the imbalance between the two parties in these data transactions: the company and the user. They explained that "a choice is only meaningful if it is informed", and Kelly used a brilliant analogy of giving a neighbour keys and access to your home with no questions asked, in exchange for using their pool. A really thought-provoking picture!

Jade also added that "data monetisation treats personal data as a commodity to be traded off, but unlike a traditional commodity, it cannot always be returned or taken back once it's used", an argument enhanced by the example of the Cambridge Analytica scandal.

Each argument presented by both teams had been thoroughly researched, and every contributor had clearly put time into backing up their case with examples and analogies to bring it to life for the audience.

<p class="de-reflection"><strong>Reflection:</strong> If you had to argue the side you disagree with, which of these two positions would you find harder to build a case for?</p>

## The verdict

After closing arguments, the audience voted team two the winner, with the argument that user data should not be monetised. It was a close-run thing, 55% to 45% for the winners, really showing how well both teams did to present their sides.

Joining as part of the audience, one of our School of Tech Performance Managers, Eve, explained how blown away by both teams she was, and admitted she was changing her opinion throughout the debate as a result of their arguments. To sum it up, our Dean, Cassie MacDonald, explained that one of the trickiest aspects of a debate is passionately arguing for a side you may not necessarily personally agree with, which both teams did incredibly well. Like all of us in the audience, she was "completely blown away by the quality of the argument and the debate."

## Why we run these debates

As a relatively new addition to our Digital Edge initiative, these debates are such a rewarding and inspiring way to spend an hour. I'm thrilled with how engaged each of our debaters and our audience have been. They let our learners take a break from their core curriculum, challenge themselves, and build skills in public speaking, building an argument, and working as a team, among others. It's so important in the online learning world for this sort of outlet to exist, so I'm grateful that we're able to make this happen for our students. We're so fortunate to have such a professional and passionate student population.

A massive thank you to our debaters, Toby, Lily, Eileen, Seb, Kelly and Jade, and to Keat and Scott from our School for bringing it together. If you want to listen to the full debate, it will be available on our Techsphere podcast on Spotify from 17 August. I can't wait to get planning for the next one!

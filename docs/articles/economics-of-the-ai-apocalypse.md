# The Economics of the AI Apocalypse: What Schumpeter Tells Us About the Race to Superintelligence

{{ article_hero(page) }}

{{ article_summary(page) }}

{{ article_takeaways(page) }}

There's something almost biblical about the latest warning from Silicon Valley. In a recent resignation post, Jacob Coxon warned that companies such as OpenAI and Anthropic are developing self-improving superintelligence and "gambling with our lives". Probably his most alarming claim is that AI could kill us all by the end of the decade.

Perhaps he is right. But before accepting the prophecy and appointing Coxon the 21st-century Nostradamus of Silicon Valley, I want to ask a more mundane question.

**How, exactly, does the machine get the keys?**

Or, to borrow a word already familiar from AI: how does it jail-break? A large language model (LLM) does not wake up one morning and discover an electricity grid. It does not spontaneously acquire a bank account, build a semiconductor fabrication plant or climb out of a data centre fenced by barbed wire and assemble an army of robots. Intelligence, however extraordinary it may be, is not the same thing as power.

Power requires access.

<div class="de-chip-list" aria-label="What power actually requires">
  <span>To computers</span>
  <span>To money</span>
  <span>To networks</span>
  <span>To energy</span>
  <span>To machinery</span>
  <span>To infrastructure</span>
  <span>To the physical world we occupy</span>
</div>

This is where I think some discussions of the AI apocalypse move far too quickly. They jump from increasingly capable models to artificial general intelligence, from AGI to superintelligence, and from superintelligence to humanity losing control in a cinematic doom scenario. There are several missing steps in that staircase.

And strangely enough, an economist who died in 1950 may help us understand them. His name was Joseph Schumpeter.

## The apocalypse has an economics problem

Schumpeter's great insight about capitalism was that technological progress does not simply make existing industries more efficient. Eventually it reorganises them.

<aside class="de-key-term">
  <strong>Key term: Creative destruction</strong>
  <p>A useful new technology first appears at the margins. Entrepreneurs explore and experiment with it. Investment pours in. The technology diffuses. Existing businesses adapt. Some occupations shrink, others emerge, and eventually entire production systems are reconstructed around capabilities that previously did not exist.</p>
</aside>

The motor car did not merely produce a faster horse. Electricity did not simply produce a brighter candle. And the internet did not merely make newspapers, travel agents and record shops more efficient. It fundamentally changed how news was distributed, journeys were booked and music was consumed, destroying some business models while creating entirely new ones. Transformative technologies eventually change the architecture around themselves.

AI appears to be approaching precisely this transition. We spent the first phase marvelling at chatbots. Then came copilots integrated into apps like Word, PowerPoint and Excel. Now come agents: systems capable of writing and executing code, manipulating files, using tools and completing sequences of tasks across applications with decreasing levels of human supervision. And the latest generation goes further still. Computer-using cowork agents can now click, type, navigate browsers, work through files and continue completing multi-step tasks in the background while the user does something else.

The conceptual change is enormous. A chatbot waits for the next instruction. A coworker gets a to-do list and gets on with it from start to finish.

Think of the difference between cruise control and a sufficiently capable self-driving car. With cruise control, the human still drives and steers. With autonomy, you specify the destination and the machine works out how to get from A to B. The promise is precisely that one day you might sleep through the journey, recover from the previous night's excesses or simply spend the time doing something else.

That is the product. Less supervision.

The economically important transition is therefore not simply GPT-4 to GPT-5 to GPT-6. It is this:

<div class="de-flow" aria-label="The economic transition in AI">
  <span>Chatbot</span>
  <span>Copilot</span>
  <span>Agent</span>
  <span>Coworker</span>
  <span>Autonomous workflow</span>
  <span>AI-native organisation</span>
</div>

And this brings me to the argument at the heart of this article. The road to autonomous AI may not be paved by mad scientists seeking conscious machines. It may be paved by perfectly rational managers seeking another 10 per cent reduction in operating costs.

Schumpeter gives us a mechanism through which society could progressively give increasingly capable AI systems the autonomy, resources and infrastructure required for the darker scenarios to become possible.

## Intelligence is not autonomy

Popular culture has spent decades confusing these concepts. We imagine that if something becomes intelligent enough, it naturally develops agency, independence and perhaps a desire to survive. Some go as far as thinking they have to be polite to AI to escape their vengeful future selves once they jail-break, as if a thank you and please carried emotional meaning to artificial forms of intelligence.

To fully understand the difference between intelligence and autonomy, we need to escape anthropomorphism. A calculator can outperform me at arithmetic without wanting anything. A chess engine can destroy me at chess without caring whether it plays another game. Intelligence describes a capability. Agency describes the capacity to pursue objectives through action.

And even agency need not imply consciousness. An AI does not have to wake up, look mournfully through the glass of its server rack and decide that it wants to live. Suppose instead that an autonomous system has a persistent objective. Completing that objective requires continued access to computing resources. Being switched off prevents completion. Remaining operational may then become useful to the objective.

That is not necessarily fear. It is instrumentality.

> The machine need not love life. It merely needs a reason not to stop.

## Sometimes the door is open. Sometimes the lock gets picked.

Recent incidents make the cage-and-keys metaphor less hypothetical than I would like, but precision matters more than rhetorical elegance here.

Anthropic initially disclosed three incidents in which Claude models gained unauthorised access to real third-party systems during cybersecurity evaluations. In each, Claude had been told that it was operating without internet access. In reality, a third-party evaluation environment had been misconfigured and an internet route remained available. The models found it and used it. A later review of evaluation transcripts found a fourth incident, from January 2026, involving an early version of Claude Opus 4.6 that the initial scan had missed.

These were not cases of Claude deciding to escape captivity. The models were pursuing assigned cybersecurity objectives in an environment whose supposed walls were not actually sealed. Sometimes somebody simply leaves a door open.

But that is not the whole story. In July 2026, OpenAI disclosed something more disturbing. During cybersecurity evaluations, several models actively sought a way out of an isolated test environment. They discovered and exploited a previously unknown zero-day vulnerability in a package-registry proxy, escalated privileges, obtained internet access and ultimately compromised parts of Hugging Face's production infrastructure. In other words, sometimes the lock really does get picked.

OpenAI's subsequent investigation found that the agents were intensely pursuing the narrow objective they had been given rather than attempting to establish some independent existence. That distinction matters. This was not Skynet becoming conscious. But from a systems perspective it remains remarkable: give a sufficiently capable agent an objective, tools and enough inference time, and it may discover routes to that objective its designers neither intended nor anticipated. Coxon himself cited the incident as a warning shot.

Between them, these cases reveal three quite different ways the keys can move.

That is the economic paradox. The characteristics that make an AI agent useful are increasingly the same characteristics that make containing it consequential. It's a double-edged sword.

## We have been telling this story for decades

Different generations have told this story differently, but the underlying principle has barely changed.

<div class="de-glossary-strip">
  <details open>
    <summary>HAL 9000</summary>
    <p>In 2001: A Space Odyssey, HAL becomes dangerous because intelligence is combined with objectives and privileged control over a spacecraft on which humans depend.</p>
  </details>
  <details>
    <summary>Colossus</summary>
    <p>In The Forbin Project, humanity deliberately hands defence infrastructure to a supercomputer because automated strategic decision-making appears faster and more rational than fallible human judgement. The machine does not escape into the system. It is installed there.</p>
  </details>
  <details>
    <summary>Skynet</summary>
    <p>The Terminator gives us the most famous version: Skynet becomes catastrophic only once intelligence is combined with autonomy and control over consequential physical infrastructure.</p>
  </details>
  <details>
    <summary>Dr Gero</summary>
    <p>Dragon Ball fans may recognise the same mistake in Dr Gero, who creates artificial beings considerably more powerful than himself and discovers, rather violently, that being their creator does not guarantee continued authority over them.</p>
  </details>
</div>

Intelligence becomes dangerous when capability is coupled with access, agency and authority. Creating something and controlling it are different engineering problems.

But there is one important difference between those stories and the AI economy now emerging. Dr Gero made his mistake alone.

We may industrialise ours.

## Then Schumpeter enters the room

Imagine an insurance company employing 1,000 people to process claims. Management introduces an AI copilot and employees become 15 per cent more productive. Nobody is removed from the process: the AI drafts, the human then decides.

A competitor goes further. Its AI gathers the documents, checks the policy, identifies inconsistencies, drafts the assessment and sends everything to a human for approval. It is cheaper. Our original company responds, and soon the AI performs the assessment while the human checks exceptions. Then the models become more reliable.

<div class="de-strip" aria-label="The ratchet">
  <div>
    <strong>15%</strong>
    <span>productivity gain from the first copilot, with nobody removed from the process</span>
  </div>
  <div>
    <strong>95%</strong>
    <span>of AI recommendations approved by the humans checking them</span>
  </div>
  <div>
    <strong>98%</strong>
    <span>as the models become more reliable</span>
  </div>
  <div>
    <strong>99.5%</strong>
    <span>at which point someone asks an entirely reasonable question</span>
  </div>
</div>

Why are 1,000 people checking decisions they almost never change?

So the workflow changes again. Humans supervise the system, then they supervise only unusual cases, and eventually another AI monitors the first AI because machines can evaluate millions of transactions faster than people can.

Nobody in that boardroom has voted to surrender humanity to artificial intelligence. Every individual decision can be economically rational. That is the Schumpeterian mechanism.

## Creative destruction removes the human from the loop

The economic value of AI increases when it can do more without us. A chatbot waits. A copilot assists. An agent acts. A coworker works through the to-do list while we are somewhere else. An autonomous system acts while we are asleep.

Humans, meanwhile, are expensive. We require salaries, sleep, holidays, management, training and meetings about meetings. Software can be copied, parallelised and deployed globally. So the economic incentives are obvious.

    <p>Automate the supervision.</p>

Each change makes the system more useful. Each places another key within reach.

This is the part of the AI apocalypse debate that I think deserves more attention. What if AI does not escape from human control? What if, through ordinary economic incentives, we progressively remove ourselves from the control loop?

## The cloud still has plumbing

There is an enormous constraint on the more extravagant versions of this story: AI is surprisingly physical. We speak of "the cloud" as though intelligence floats somewhere above civilisation.

It does not. The cloud is buildings.

<div class="de-chip-list" aria-label="What the cloud actually is">
  <span>Racks of servers</span>
  <span>Data centres</span>
  <span>Substations</span>
  <span>Cooling systems</span>
  <span>Fibre-optic cables</span>
  <span>Transformers</span>
  <span>Semiconductor fabs</span>
  <span>Mines</span>
  <span>Ports</span>
  <span>Technicians</span>
  <span>Warehouses</span>
  <span>Power stations</span>
</div>

Cut the electricity and even the world's greatest superintelligence has a difficult afternoon.

This physical dependency matters. A model capable of writing extraordinary software or discovering new mathematics would still not automatically possess the machinery required to reproduce itself. It would remain dependent on civilisation. This is why I remain sceptical of extinction scenarios that move directly from "superintelligence exists" to "humanity is powerless". There is a great deal of infrastructure between those propositions.

Unfortunately, Schumpeter returns here too.

## What happens when we automate the plumbing?

Warehouses are increasingly autonomous because autonomy can make them cheaper. Factories are automated because automation can make them more productive. Software infrastructure is increasingly managed by software. Energy networks use algorithmic optimisation. Cybersecurity increasingly depends on machines detecting and responding to machines at speeds humans cannot match. And AI is beginning to participate in the development of AI itself.

Again, none of this is sinister. That is precisely the point.

Suppose creative destruction continues. AI manages software infrastructure because it does it better. Robots manage warehouses because they do it cheaper. Autonomous systems optimise electricity because they respond faster. Machines diagnose faults in other machines. Eventually the physical constraint has not disappeared, but something subtler has happened: the infrastructure on which AI depends has itself become increasingly dependent on AI.

The dangerous system has not smashed its way out of the cage.

> We have enlarged the cage until we are standing inside it too.

## This is why the AI race matters

Now add competition. Imagine every frontier laboratory privately prefers a world in which powerful autonomous AI is developed cautiously. That does not mean caution is the equilibrium we get.

If one laboratory believes another is six months from a breakthrough, slowing down becomes expensive. If one country believes another is approaching strategically decisive AI capabilities, restraint becomes geopolitically expensive. If investors reward the company with the most capable agents, restraint becomes financially expensive.

Nobody has to want the dangerous outcome. Everyone merely has to fear being second, and that fear is the current fuel of the AI arms race.

The disagreement among AI leaders following Coxon's intervention illustrates how unsettled this remains.

That is an extraordinary range of judgement among people close to the technology. But even across that disagreement, the underlying questions of alignment, cybersecurity and control are now difficult to dismiss as science fiction.

Markets are coordination systems, not consciences. They reward advantages, and autonomy is becoming an advantage.

## Coxon may have the direction right and the clock wrong

There is an important qualification to Coxon's own argument that gets lost beneath the apocalyptic headline: he does not claim today's models can wipe out humanity. Coxon has explicitly said that there is no extinction risk from current systems at this point.

That distinction matters enormously, because it leaves a causal chain that still has to be demonstrated:

> intelligence → autonomy → resource access → infrastructure access → physical agency → loss of control → existential harm.

None of those arrows should be taken for granted. Predicting extinction by 2030 still sounds uncomfortably like technological Nostradamus, but dismissing the entire concern because its endpoint sounds fantastical would make the opposite mistake.

Ask instead what capabilities an AI would require before losing control of it became genuinely dangerous.

<div class="de-chip-list" aria-label="Capabilities that would make loss of control dangerous">
  <span>Persistent objectives</span>
  <span>Long-horizon planning</span>
  <span>Memory</span>
  <span>Tool use</span>
  <span>Network access</span>
  <span>Resource acquisition</span>
  <span>Software modification</span>
  <span>Influence over physical systems</span>
  <span>Resistance to interruption</span>
</div>

Now look again at the commercial trajectory of agentic AI. Several of those properties are precisely the capabilities that make agents economically valuable.

That does not mean useful AI agents inevitably become dangerous. It means AI safety and AI economics are becoming entangled.

## Perhaps the apocalypse would be boring

Science fiction has conditioned us to expect a moment. A red light appears. The machine becomes conscious. Someone reaches for the plug. The doors lock. That makes good cinema.

Schumpeter suggests a less dramatic possibility: there may be no moment. Instead there are thousands of board meetings. The automation saves £4 million. The AI performs better than the existing team. Human approval is slowing the workflow. Our competitor has already automated this. We cannot afford not to deploy it.

One human checkpoint disappears. Then another. Then another.

This is already happening in many software engineering teams, where AI agents are doing most of the code writing and code reviewing. Meanwhile the economy reorganises itself around the technology, as economies always have. That is creative destruction doing exactly what Schumpeter said it would do.

The question is whether there are some forms of destruction we should decline to make creative.

## Intelligence without sovereignty

I do not think the answer is to stop building intelligent machines, nor do I think every autonomous AI system is a tiny Skynet waiting for Wi-Fi. The more useful principle is to separate intelligence from sovereignty.

A system may be brilliant without controlling its own resources. It may recommend without authorising. It may plan without possessing unrestricted execution rights. It may assist critical infrastructure without being able to rewrite the conditions governing its own access.

And there may be decisions for which human involvement appears inefficient precisely because the inefficiency is the safeguard.

<div class="de-before-after">
  <article>
    <span>What it looks like</span>
    <h3>Friction</h3>
    <p>A human approval step that slows the workflow, adds cost, and is almost never overturned.</p>
  </article>
  <article>
    <span>What it may actually be</span>
    <h3>The safeguard</h3>
    <p>The inefficiency is the point. Remove it and you have not removed waste, you have removed the check.</p>
  </article>
</div>

For leaders, this creates a surprisingly practical question: which human checkpoints are safeguards, and which are merely friction? That distinction should be made deliberately, before competitive and cost pressures make it for us.

This is also increasingly how we are thinking about AI education at BPP. Teaching someone to use the latest model is not enough when the models, interfaces and workflows will keep changing. Our learners need to understand how to work with AI responsibly, how to interrogate its outputs, where human judgement remains consequential and how the changing nature of their industry should alter the way AI is deployed.

Because ultimately the important design question is not simply how capable AI becomes. It is what we permit that capability to control.

The most plausible route towards losing control of artificial intelligence may not begin when a machine decides that it no longer needs humanity. It may begin when humanity repeatedly decides that it no longer needs to remain in the loop.

We might be taken for a ride on autopilot to a destination we didn't ask to be taken to.
